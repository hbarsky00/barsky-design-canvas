A case study is a record of decisions, not a record of work

By Hiram Barsky•October 27, 2026•6 min read
PortfolioCareerWritingCase Studies

Most portfolio case studies describe a process — discover, define, design, deliver — and never say what was actually at stake. Here is the structure that forces the other thing out.

Here is the what a portfolio proves now almost everyone writes. The client had a problem. I did discovery. I made personas and a journey map. I wireframed, then I designed, then I tested. Here are twelve screens. The client was happy.

Everything in it is true and none of it is information. A reader finishes knowing your method and nothing about your judgement, and method is the part that is now freely available to anyone who asks for it.

The question every reader is actually asking

Whoever is reading — a hiring manager, a founder deciding whether to call you — is running one test: when this person hit something genuinely unclear, what did they do? That's it. Everything else is context for that question.

Which means the load-bearing part of a case study is the moment two reasonable options conflicted and you had to pick. Not the cost-effective user research methods that confirmed what you expected. The research that contradicted it, and what you did about that on a Tuesday with a deadline on Friday.

If nothing in your case study surprised you at the time, you have written a summary of a plan, not an account of a project.

A structure that makes the specifics unavoidable

What was actually at stake. Not "improve the user experience". Who was losing what, measured how, and what happened to the business if it stayed broken. If you cannot say this, you were not close enough to the project to write it up — which is worth knowing before you spend a weekend on the screens.

The constraint. Every real project has one that shaped everything: a legacy system, a regulator, a two-person team, a date that could not move. A case study with no constraint reads as fiction, because readers who have shipped anything know the constraint is where the design actually happened.

The decision. One specific fork. Both options, honestly described, including why the one you rejected was tempting. Then the call and the reasoning. This is the section nobody writes and the only one anybody remembers.

What it cost. What got worse because of your choice. Every real decision trades something away. Naming the trade is the single strongest credibility signal available to you, and it is free.

What you would do differently. Specific, not performed humility. "I'd run the research two weeks earlier" is useful. "I learned so much from this project" is not.

On the writing itself

Write it the way you would explain it to a peer over coffee who keeps asking "wait, why?" That voice is impatient, concrete and allergic to process vocabulary, which is exactly right. Then cut every sentence that would be true of any project. "We aligned on goals" is true of every project. Delete it.

And if you can generate a draft in a minute — you can, and so can everyone — notice what that means. Fluent prose has stopped being evidence of anything. The only part of your case study that is scarce is the part only you were in the room for. Spend your effort there and let the rest be plain.

The test before you publish

Read it back and ask: could a competent stranger have written this from the brief alone, without having done the project? If yes, you have written a plan. The fix is never more polish. It is one honest paragraph about something that went sideways.
