Ship something small: the fastest way to grow in the AI era

By Hiram Barsky•March 11, 2026•3 min read
Workflow

Not a course. Not a certification. The fastest way to grow as a designer or developer right now is to ship one small thing end to end — and AI just removed every excuse.

Every designer I talk to is asking the same question, just in different words: how do I stay valuable while AI-enhanced UX design eats the routine parts of my job? Everyone’s answer is a course, a certificate, a prompt-engineering listicle. Skip all of it. The highest-return move on the table costs nothing and takes a couple weekends: pick something embarrassingly small and ship it — end to end, all the way to a URL a stranger can actually open.

Why shipping beats studying

A course teaches you a tool. Shipping teaches you judgment — and judgment is the part AI can’t commoditize. Take even a tiny product from idea to live and you’re forced through every decision a real product demands: what to cut, what 'done' means, how it should feel, what happens when it breaks, what to call it, how anyone finds it. No tutorial simulates that, because the whole point is living with your own tradeoffs. And AI makes it newly doable solo — it generates the scaffolding, drafts the copy, writes the code you couldn’t, while every decision that makes the thing good stays yours. That split is exactly the skill the next decade pays for.

Keep the scope embarrassingly small

The projects that die don’t die from missing talent. They die of fog. If the core of your idea doesn’t fit in one sentence, it’s not scoped yet. A calculator for a workflow you already know. A one-loop game. A tool your team keeps wishing existed. Small isn’t a compromise — it’s the strategy. AI’s speed compounds when the vision is sharp and evaporates when you spend it wandering around in a haze. My own proof run was [a pair of tiny browser games](/case-studies/fire-lion). Nothing ambitious. Everything instructive.

AI doesn’t remove the gap between concept and shipped. It removes the excuse for not crossing it.

Ship to the web, share the link

Where you ship is a design decision, and for a solo builder there’s one right answer: the open web. A link is the whole onboarding — no install, no app-store review, no update cycle. It collapses the distance between 'I made a thing' and 'a stranger is using it,' and that second state is where all the learning lives. Real people typing nonsense into your product at 11pm will teach you more in a week than a year of polishing shots nobody uses.

Pick something small enough to actually finish

The mistake isn’t picking something too hard. It’s picking something too big, stalling at sixty percent, and quietly concluding you’re not the kind of person who ships. Small means one screen doing one job, with real data, deployed at a URL a stranger can open. That’s it. Small enough that the interesting problems are in the last ten percent instead of the first eighty. A reminder that actually fires. A calculator that does one calculation properly. Not a platform. Definitely not a marketplace. If you can’t describe it in one sentence without using the word “and,” it’s too big for a first one.

The ugly last ten percent is the entire lesson

The tutorial ends when the happy path works. Your education starts right after that. What shows when there’s no data yet. What it says when the request fails. What it does on a phone in one hand. What happens when someone taps twice, or does the steps out of order, or leaves and comes back an hour later. Every one of those is a decision nobody can make for you, and none of them appear in the course you were thinking of buying. Ship one small thing end to end and you’ll have made those decisions for real — which is the difference between having an opinion about product work and having done it.

What it does for your UX job search strategies

The first thing you ship changes how every room sees you. You stop being the person who describes products and become the person who makes them exist — in interviews, in client calls, in the meeting where someone asks 'could we even build that?' and you’re the one who already knows. Your what a portfolio proves now stops being pictures of intentions and starts being links that work. In a market where everyone’s résumé says 'AI,' a URL that actually does something is the one credential nobody can fake.
