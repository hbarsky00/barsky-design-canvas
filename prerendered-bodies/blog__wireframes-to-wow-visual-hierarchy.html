Generated layouts all have the same problem

By Hiram Barsky•October 6, 2026•6 min read
Visual DesignAIInterface DesignCraft

Ask for a page and you get something clean, balanced, and completely flat — every element weighted the same. Hierarchy is the judgement that didn't come in the box.

Generate a landing page and look at what comes back. The type scale is sensible, the spacing is consistent, the colours don't fight. And every section is shouting at exactly the same volume, so your eye lands nowhere in particular and slides off.

This is the consistent failure mode, and it is structural rather than a quality problem. Hierarchy is a statement about what matters most, and nothing in the request said what mattered most. So you get the average: everything slightly emphasised, which is identical to nothing emphasised.

Hierarchy is a decision about the business

Deciding the primary action on a page means deciding what this page is for, which means deciding what the product is trying to get someone to do, which is a product question wearing a visual costume. That is why it can't be inferred from "make a pricing page". The designer who knows that most visitors arrive confused about which plan they need will build a completely different page from the one who knows most arrive ready to buy the middle tier.

Three emphasised things on a page is zero emphasised things with extra steps.

The edit that fixes most of it

Take the generated page and pick one element — exactly one — that a visitor must see. Then systematically weaken everything else. Not strengthen the one; weaken the rest. Drop the secondary button to a text link. Take the third heading down two steps. Remove the icon that was decorating rather than clarifying.

This is almost always subtraction, which is why it rarely survives a generated draft: the draft is additive by construction, and every element in it looks deliberate because it is rendered as carefully as every other element.

Where it gets expensive

Dashboards and data-dense screens, because the flattening is harder to see. Twelve equally-weighted metric tiles look organised. They are organised the way a filing cabinet is organised — technically correct, and no help at all to someone who needs to know whether today is going badly. The design work is deciding which two numbers get to be large, and that requires knowing what the person opening it is afraid of.

Same in long forms. A generated form treats every field as equally important because the schema did. The human version knows that two of those fields cause most of the abandonment and everything else is noise around them.

Why this is good news

For years, a lot of visual design labour went into producing the competent baseline — the consistent spacing, the sane type scale, the colours that work. That is now free and it is genuinely better than what most teams were shipping.

What remains is the part that was always the interesting half: knowing what this particular screen is for, and being willing to make everything else on it quieter. That has not got easier, it has just stopped being hidden underneath the production work.
