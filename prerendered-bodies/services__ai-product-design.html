AI product design is the design of a product whose core value comes from a model — covering the interaction model, the interface, and the AI’s behavior when it is confident, uncertain, or wrong, not just the screens around it.

When this is the problem

  • Your demo lands in the room and then stalls, because the demo used an input somebody chose and real people don’t.
  • The model works, but nobody can tell when to trust an answer — so they check everything, which is slower than not using it.
  • Engineers are making product decisions by default, because nobody has defined what should happen when confidence is low.

What it includes

The interaction model
How a person and the model take turns, how context accumulates, and where the human stays in control. This is the part that decides whether your product feels like a tool or a slot machine.
Behavior design for confident, uncertain and wrong
The three states every AI product has and most only design for one of. What it shows while thinking, how it signals shaky ground, how someone corrects it without starting over, and when it should refuse outright instead of guessing.
The trust surface
Sources shown next to answers rather than behind a click, so a person can verify in one glance and defend the decision to their boss. Verifiable beats authoritative.
The interface itself
Screens, states, and the full set nobody demos: empty, loading, error, offline, and the one where the user did it in the wrong order.

How it runs

  1. 1

    Frame

    We look at real model output at volume — hundreds of rows, not the six that made the deck. You cannot design for behavior you have not watched.

  2. 2

    Design

    Interaction model and interface, designed from the ugliest realistic input rather than the clean one. If it holds there, the clean case takes care of itself.

  3. 3

    Ship

    Built in React and TypeScript, so the engagement can end in working software instead of a file someone still has to interpret.

  4. 4

    Measure

    Watch where people hesitate, correct, or abandon. Those three moments tell you more than any satisfaction score.

What an engagement looks like

Most AI product design engagements run four to ten weeks, depending on whether we are shaping something new or repairing a product that demos well and stalls with real users. Project-based or retainer. The first thirty-minute call is free and is where scope gets defined — you leave it with a clear picture of the work whether or not you hire us.

Not for: This is the wrong service if the AI in your product is genuinely incidental — a smart filter bolted onto a stable core, where the value would survive removing it. That is ordinary product design and you should pay ordinary product design prices for it. It is also wrong if what you actually need is a model that performs better; we design behavior around a model, we do not train one. And if the goal is a demo for a funding round rather than something people will use on a Tuesday, say so up front, because the work is different and cheaper.

Questions

What makes AI product design different from regular UX?
Traditional UX assumes a deterministic system: press the button, get the same result every time. An AI product is probabilistic, so the design has to answer questions conventional interface work never raises — how much to trust any single answer, what shows while the model is thinking, how someone corrects a wrong result without losing their work, and what happens when the model is confidently incorrect. The screens can look identical. The behavior underneath is a different discipline.
Do you need our model to be finished first?
No, and it is usually better if it isn’t. Design decisions change what the model needs to do — how fast it must respond, what it should refuse, what it has to cite. Bringing design in after the model is frozen means designing around choices that were never examined.
Can you build it, or only design it?
Both. We build in React and TypeScript, which is the point of the studio. The design is made by the person who has to implement it, so it accounts for real constraints — latency, failure, cost — instead of discovering them late.
